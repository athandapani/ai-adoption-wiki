# Change Management for AI Adoption: A 2026 Playbook

**Author:** Digital Applied Team
**Published:** 2026-06-14

## ADKAR applied to AI

| Stage | In AI context | Common stall | Tactic | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Understanding why AI is introduced and what problem it solves | Low comprehension causes early disengagement | Lead with business problem, not tool; name what stays human | Hallway questions about job risk |
| Desire | Individual can answer WIIFM for their specific role | Broadcast comms miss individual motivations | Role-specific WIIFM; surface peer wins | High shadow-AI usage |
| Knowledge | People know prompting, output limits, when to trust | Most workers receive zero employer AI training | Role-specific training, prompt libraries, office hours | Usage spikes then collapses |
| Ability | Real workflow application under normal pressure | Tool works in demos but fails in practice | Embed in existing workflow; pair with champions | Workarounds and manual fallbacks |
| Reinforcement | Use sustained beyond launch | Adoption treated as project with an end date | Measure adoption not deployment; recognize champions | Usage drifts down post-rollout |

## Wage premium (verbatim)

"Workers with AI skills command roughly a 62% wage premium per PwC, up from 57% the prior year."

## Champion network

Peer champions run office hours and answer practical "how do I" questions that broadcast
communications cannot address. Microsoft achieved "~90% monthly active usage" through
role-specific training, gamification, and peer-to-peer learning networks rather than top-down
mandates.

## Core argument

Adoption succeeds **person-by-person** through visible sponsorship, role-specific enablement,
honest communication about job displacement, and continuous measurement of behavioral change —
not license deployment. Technology is commoditized; organizational capacity to adopt well is the
durable competitive advantage.
