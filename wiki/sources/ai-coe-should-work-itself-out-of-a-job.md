## Body

**Author:** Amit Kothari.

The argument: CoEs are **temporary scaffolding, not permanent foundations**.

> "Real success is that the team's knowledge lives everywhere. The people move on to other roles,
> but the capability stays."

**An 18-month timeline with "an end date from day one."**

**Drucker**, paraphrased from *The Effective Executive*: "The best organizations push capability to
where the work happens, not keep it locked in a central group."

Statistics:
- "**95 percent of generative AI pilots** at companies failing to achieve rapid revenue acceleration"
- "Two-thirds of workers say their organization has not been proactive in AI training and upskilling"
- **"58% use AI at work and 57% hide it"**
- "Very few organizations qualify as AI high performers seeing real bottom-line impact"

Mechanism: rotations, peer-to-peer learning, and documented frameworks that business teams own
independently.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

**The playbook keeps the sentiment and drops the deadline.** Section 11.1 cites the Drucker insight
and "an AI CoE that becomes a permanent gatekeeper has failed its mission"; Section 12.2 says "make
the CoE invisible over time". Neither mentions the **18-month end date** — which is the only
operable part. Every CoE has always intended to dissolve.

**"58% use AI at work and 57% hide it"** sits inside the playbook's own CoE source and is never
connected to its Section 4.5 shadow-AI material. Nor is the **95%-of-pilots-fail** figure, which
also appears in ref [^37].

Where the dissolution argument is weakest: model risk management, EU AI Act conformity assessment,
and vendor subprocessor review are specialist functions with regulatory teeth — €35M or 7% of global
revenue, plus California's 2028 personal criminal liability. "Push it to where the work happens" is
a poor answer to an obligation requiring a named accountable expert. Some of it should stay central.

The 18-month figure has no derivation. A personal blog by an AI consultant.
