## Body

**Author:** Victor Hoang, Co-Founder, Rework.com.

The refinement that improves on the playbook's version: AI literacy is not one skill but **four
distinct competencies** — prompt engineering, output verification, escalation judgment, policy
awareness — calibrated by role. ICs focus on prompting and verification; managers add escalation
judgment and workflow redesign; executives add investment strategy and risk governance.

**Output verification** is "the least visible but most consequential gap", because employees
intuitively trust confident-sounding outputs without verification habits. That connects directly to
[[ai-hallucination-risk|hallucination risk]]: NIST's definition is "*confidently stated* but false
content", and the confidence is what defeats untrained readers.

Statistics: "59% of the global workforce will require training by 2030... yet only 35% of
organizations currently have a mature, workforce-wide upskilling program" (WEF/DataCamp);
"Organizations with formal AI training programs achieve 2.3x faster AI adoption and 67% higher AI
ROI" (attributed to OECD); "42% of employees say their employer expects them to learn AI on their
own, while 34% report feeling unprepared" (DataCamp 2026).

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

**The "2.3x faster adoption, 67% higher ROI" figure is attributed to the OECD and does not appear in
this vault's OECD capture.** A vendor page attributing precise multipliers to a named institution,
with no link, is exactly the pattern that produced the playbook's other attribution failures. Treat
as unverified.

Rework.com sells AI transformation services, and the article's conclusion is that organizations need
structured AI training programs.

The four-competency model itself has no validation data — it's a well-reasoned taxonomy, not a
finding. But it's a better taxonomy than the playbook's flat curriculum, and the verification
insight is the most useful claim in the change-management material.
