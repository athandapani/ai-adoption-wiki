## Body

**Author:** Ajay Pundhir.

- **"95% of enterprise AI pilots are failing to deliver measurable business returns"** (MIT NANDA
  initiative)
- **"76% of executives believe their employees feel enthusiastic about AI adoption. The reality?
  Just 31%"** (BCG/Columbia Business School)
- "31% of employees admit to actively undermining their company's AI rollout" (Writer survey)
- "42% of C-suite executives report that AI adoption is tearing their company apart" (Writer)

**Four types of resistance** — a taxonomy of the *resistance* rather than the person, and more
actionable than archetypes: **tool** (technology limitations), **strategy** (deployment
misalignment), **trust** (credibility issues), **capability** (insufficient training). Each names a
fix.

Its thesis: resistance is organizational intelligence, not obstruction. Companies that empower
domain experts to surface problems outperform those imposing solutions top-down.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

**The playbook cites this source once, for four words** — "treat resistance as signal, not noise" in
Section 8.4 — and takes nothing else.

It omits **95% of pilots failing** (MIT NANDA), which also appears in ref [^43]. Two of the
playbook's own sources carry it; it appears nowhere in the playbook, which instead reports "85%
saying AI has exceeded expectations".

It omits the **76%/31% perception gap**, which is the finding that most damages its own Section 1.3
conclusion. That conclusion — "barriers are structural and resource-based, not attitudinal" — rests
on self-reported resistance figures of 6–7%. If executives overestimate enthusiasm by 45 points,
self-reported resistance data is exactly the instrument you cannot trust. See
[[ai-adoption-barriers|Barriers to AI Adoption]].

Forbes Tech Council is a paid membership program; contributor posts are not editorially reviewed
Forbes journalism. The MIT NANDA and Writer figures are cited but not linked.
