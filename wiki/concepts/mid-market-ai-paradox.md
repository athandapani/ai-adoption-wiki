## Body

The structural paradox for firms in the $50M–$500M ARR band: they are large enough to generate
significant institutional knowledge and complex operational data, yet lean enough that dedicated
AI teams, enterprise architects, and data engineering squads don't exist.

That produces a distinctive vulnerability — fragmented knowledge trapped in siloed tools,
untransferred through attrition, invisibly at risk from ungoverned AI usage — and, on the
playbook's argument, a corresponding opportunity: the knowledge asset already exists and needs
activation rather than creation.

RSM's 2025 survey of US and Canadian middle-market firms captures the shape of it: **78–92% report
using AI**, **85% say AI has exceeded expectations**, and **54% found deployment harder than
anticipated**. High adoption, high satisfaction, high difficulty — simultaneously. **67% say they
need external help** with strategy and governance, which is the paradox stated as a purchasing
decision.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

The adoption figures underpinning this framing don't reconcile across sources. RSM says 78–92% of
mid-market firms use AI. OECD says 39% of SMEs. Alice Labs reports 18% of firms using AI in at
least one business function as of January 2026. These describe overlapping populations with
wildly different definitions of "use AI" — anything from a licensed Copilot seat to a production
workflow. The playbook stacks them without reconciling.

"85% say AI exceeded expectations" also sits uneasily beside MIT NANDA's finding — carried by two
of the playbook's own sources — that 95% of enterprise AI pilots fail to deliver measurable
returns. Both cannot be describing the same reality. Self-reported satisfaction and measured
return are different instruments, and only one of them is in the playbook.
