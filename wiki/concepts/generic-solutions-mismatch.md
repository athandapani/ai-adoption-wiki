## Body

OECD's finding: **27% of SMEs** aware of AI solutions but not using them report that "available
supports were not adapted to their needs".

Off-the-shelf AI products fail mid-market structurally — they require the business to adapt its
processes to the technology, lack industry-specific customization, and carry hidden training and
maintenance costs. Daijobu's conclusion from the same OECD data is the same: custom, tailored
solutions beat generic off-the-shelf products for SMEs.

This is the argument for a Second Brain anchored to the organization's own knowledge rather than a
generic assistant — and it connects directly to
[[the-proprietary-knowledge-moat|The Proprietary Knowledge Moat]]: a competitor can buy the same
model but cannot replicate your accumulated institutional knowledge.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

The 27% figure is thinner than the weight placed on it. It measures *stated reason for
non-adoption among the aware-but-not-using* — a subset of a subset, self-reported. "Not adapted to
our needs" is also the most socially acceptable answer available; it attributes failure to the
vendor rather than to the respondent's budget, skills, or inertia. The same survey's cost barriers
(40% maintenance, 32% hardware) may be the real constraint wearing a nicer label.

The deeper tension: "generic solutions don't fit" sits awkwardly beside the playbook's own
build-vs-buy recommendation to **buy the commodity layer**. Every tool in its Section 10.1
comparison — Glean, Guru, Notion AI, Confluence — is a generic solution. The playbook recommends
buying them. The reconciliation it intends is that genericism is fine for infrastructure and fatal
for the knowledge layer, but it never says so, and the two sections read as contradictory.
