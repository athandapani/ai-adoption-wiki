## Body

A 2025 Academy of Management study, via Alice Labs: when supervisors **co-present AI-generated
feedback** — walking through outputs with their team rather than delegating the AI interaction
entirely — employee resistance drops measurably.

**What managers should do:** open team meetings with an AI-assisted summary; reference AI analysis
in decisions explicitly; invite teams to challenge AI outputs, normalizing critical engagement.

**What managers should not do:** send AI-generated feedback without a conversation; use AI outputs
as final authority rather than input; announce AI adoption without demonstrating personal usage.

The mechanism is legible: co-presenting makes the human accountable for the output. Delegating the
interaction makes the AI the author, which is both a trust problem and an accountability vacuum.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

**The WEF study complicates this considerably.** It found that even "AI enthusiasts" tasked with
implementing AI strategies "often privately doubt their effectiveness", and describes a
**frontstage/backstage split** where public compliance masks private circumvention. If that runs
through the management layer — and nothing suggests it stops below it — then "managers visibly use
AI in meetings" prescribes exactly the frontstage performance the WEF identifies as the failure
mode. Employees are reportedly good at telling the difference.

That turns the prescription into something harder: leaders must actually find AI useful, which is
not a change-management intervention.

The playbook calls this "the single highest-leverage variable in AI adoption" — a claim no source
supports. Alice Labs reports a single study showing resistance drops when supervisors co-present.
That's one finding about one intervention, not a ranking of all variables.

The Academy of Management study is also cited second-hand; it's not named, dated, or linked in the
capture, so its design and effect size are unknown.
