## Body

Large enterprises and consultancies consistently outbid mid-market for MLOps engineers, prompt
engineers, and data scientists. Gartner's forecast of **44% growth in AI spending** (to $2.52T in
2026) drives proportional demand for AI talent, compressing availability further for mid-market
buyers. This is a structural position, not a negotiating failure — mid-market cannot win a bidding
war against firms with more capital and better AI-career narratives.

The practical response the playbook lands on is right: **AI literacy over AI specialization.**
Rather than hiring specialists, make existing employees competent users.

Forrester's finding, via Datagrom, is the damning one: **only half of AI decision-makers offer
training to non-technical employees**, and that figure grew just **4 percentage points between
2024 and 2025**. Adoption is accelerating; enablement isn't.

Corroborating figures from Victor Hoang: "59% of the global workforce will require training by
2030"; "only 35% of organizations currently have a mature, workforce-wide upskilling program";
"42% of employees say their employer expects them to learn AI on their own". And Amit Kothari:
"Two-thirds of workers say their organization has not been proactive in AI training".

Four independent sources, same shape. This is one of the better-evidenced claims in the corpus.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

"AI literacy over specialization" is sound for adoption and insufficient for building. A firm that
trains everyone to prompt well and hires nobody who can evaluate a retrieval pipeline will deploy
tools competently and be unable to tell when they're wrong. The playbook's own Section 5.3 quietly
concedes this by putting an "AI Engineer / Architect" in the minimum viable CoE — you still need
one, the literacy argument just means you need one rather than a team.

Hoang's counter is sharper: output verification is "the least visible but most consequential gap".
Literacy training that teaches prompting without teaching verification produces confident users of
unreliable outputs — arguably worse than no adoption, given
[[ai-hallucination-risk|hallucination rates]].

The Gartner-demand-compresses-supply argument is also assumed, not shown. No source in this corpus
provides mid-market AI salary or time-to-hire data.
