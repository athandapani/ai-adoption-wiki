## Body

The curriculum, from ref [^15]: **4–8 hours of contact time across 2–4 sessions**, delivered to
live cohorts of 15–25.

- **Foundations** — how generative AI works in plain English; what tokens are, why models make
  things up
- **Practical prompting** — role, context, examples, constraints, iteration
- **Risks and red lines** — hallucinations, bias, data protection, IP risk, and the organization's
  specific [[ai-acceptable-use-policy|AUP]]
- **Hands-on practice** — every learner completes a piece of their own real work during the
  session, not a sandbox exercise
- **Use-case map** — everyone leaves with 3–5 specific things they'll start doing

**The delivery rule that matters most: train leaders first or in parallel — never last.**
Organizations that delegate AI literacy to "the tech team" produce leaders who can't make informed
bets and approve initiatives based on hype rather than capability.

Victor Hoang's refinement is better than the playbook's version: AI literacy isn't one skill but
**four distinct competencies** — prompt engineering, output verification, escalation judgment, and
policy awareness — calibrated by role. ICs focus on prompting and verification; managers add
escalation judgment and workflow redesign; executives add investment strategy and risk governance.

He also names the gap everyone else misses: **output verification** is "the least visible but most
consequential", because employees intuitively trust confident-sounding outputs without verification
habits.

The payoff figures: "organizations with formal AI training programs achieve 2.3x faster AI adoption
and 67% higher AI ROI" (Hoang, attributed to OECD), and a "~62% wage premium" for AI-skilled
workers (PwC via ref [^27]) — the datapoint most likely to convert anxiety into motion.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

The 4–8 hour prescription has no evidence behind it in either source — no comparison against 2
hours or 20, no measured outcome. It's a consultant's default, and both sources sell training.

The "2.3x faster adoption, 67% higher ROI" figure is attributed to OECD by a vendor page and does
not appear in this vault's OECD capture. Treat as unverified.

Ref [^15]'s own observation about what happens *without* literacy training — "15% of staff use it
daily, 30% have logged in once, and the rest haven't touched it" — is offered as an argument for
training but is equally an argument that the tool was wrong for the work. Literacy training can't
fix a tool nobody needs.
