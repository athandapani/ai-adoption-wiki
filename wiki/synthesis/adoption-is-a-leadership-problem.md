## Body

The convergence across sources is unusually strong. Bob Marsh, summarizing BCG: "When an AI
initiative stalls, it almost never stalls because the LLM model underperformed. It stalls because
the people who were supposed to change how they work didn't change." McKinsey, via Alice Labs:
change management and organizational silos "outrank technology gaps" as the #1 barrier. Alice Labs
puts a number on it — "82% of AI adoption failures trace to change management gaps, not
technology." Amit Kothari and Ajay Pundhir both cite MIT NANDA's finding that 95% of enterprise AI
pilots fail to deliver measurable returns, and both attribute it to organizational rather than
technical causes.

Five sources, same direction. That is about as close to consensus as this corpus gets.

**The problem is that the playbook's own Section 1.3 says the opposite**, and says it first. Citing
OECD data, it reports management resistance at 7% and employee resistance at 6% — the two smallest
barriers on the list — and draws the explicit conclusion:

> "The low figures for cultural resistance are counterintuitive but important: the barriers are
> structural and resource-based, not attitudinal. This shifts the intervention model from
> persuasion campaigns to resource reallocation and build-buy-partner decisions."

Then Section 8 argues at length that adoption is fundamentally a persuasion and leadership
problem. Both sections are sourced. Neither acknowledges the other.

The resolution is probably that they measure different things. OECD asked SMEs to name barriers to
*starting* — and firms that haven't started don't yet know their people won't change. The
change-management sources measure why deployments *fail after launch*. Resistance is invisible at
the intake stage and dominant at the adoption stage. If that's right, the playbook's Section 1.3
conclusion — reallocate resources, don't run persuasion campaigns — is advice derived from the
wrong stage of the lifecycle.

Ajay Pundhir supplies the figure that should have settled it, and that the playbook omits despite
citing him: **76% of executives believe their employees feel enthusiastic about AI. Just 31%
actually are.** Self-reported barrier surveys are exactly the instrument that gap would corrupt.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

"It's a leadership problem" is unfalsifiable as usually stated. Every failed initiative can be
retrofitted to a leadership failure, which makes the claim explanatory of everything and
predictive of nothing. The Alice Labs "82% of failures trace to change management" figure has no
visible methodology in the capture, and attributing 82% of anything to a single cause should
invite scepticism.

There is also a self-serving pattern: four of the five sources making this argument sell change
management, consulting, or adoption services. "Your technology is fine, your organization needs
help" is their product.

The strongest genuine counter comes from the WEF study, which found that even AI enthusiasts
"often privately doubt their effectiveness" — suggesting the frontstage/backstage split runs
through the leadership layer too. If managers modeling adoption are themselves performing it, the
leadership-modeling prescription is thinner than it looks.

What would change this conclusion: a controlled comparison of matched deployments differing only
in leadership engagement. No source here offers one.
