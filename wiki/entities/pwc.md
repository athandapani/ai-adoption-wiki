## Body

Via ref [^27]: **"Workers with AI skills command roughly a 62% wage premium per PwC, up from 57%
the prior year."**

The playbook uses this in Section 8.4 as the answer to the "anxious" posture: a change program that
connects AI skill acquisition to personal career outcomes converts fear into motion. That's a
reasonable use — it's the only figure in the change-management material that speaks to individual
self-interest rather than organizational benefit, which is exactly what the ADKAR *Desire* stage
requires.

The year-over-year movement (57% → 62%) is the more interesting half, and the playbook drops it. A
premium that's still widening suggests the skills shortage isn't clearing.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

Read via ref [^27], a marketing agency's blog. PwC's underlying report — presumably its AI Jobs
Barometer — isn't named, dated, or linked.

The bigger issue is what a wage premium measures. It's cross-sectional: AI-skilled workers earn 62%
more. That does **not** mean an individual acquiring AI skills gains 62%. AI skills concentrate in
tech, finance, and consulting — high-paying sectors — and among younger, more educated workers. A
large share of that premium is almost certainly composition, not causation.

Using it as a motivational lever with warehouse or shop-floor staff — the workforce
[[ai-in-traditional-industries|traditional industries]] actually employ — implies a return that the
data doesn't support for those roles. The playbook offers it as the answer to job-displacement fear,
which is the context where over-promising does most damage.
