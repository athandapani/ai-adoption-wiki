## Body

Gartner figures across the playbook:

- **$2.52 trillion** global AI spending forecast for 2026, **up 44% year-over-year**
- **$6.31 trillion** total IT spending, 13.5% growth
- Software spending: $1.44T (15.1% growth); datacenter systems: $788B
- AI tools forecast to reach **12–15% of SMB software spending by 2027** (currently ~5% of SMB IT
  budgets)
- **47% of AI budgets** exceed estimates (via ref [^18])

The 44% growth figure is what drives the playbook's [[ai-skills-gap|talent-compression argument]]:
rising AI spend pulls demand for AI talent up, squeezing mid-market buyers who can't outbid.

Note the two independent global totals in this vault: Gartner says **$2.52T** (ref [^14], April
2026); AIStackHub says **$2.59T** (ref [^33], citing Gartner May 2026). Close enough to be the same
forecast revised, but worth flagging that the playbook cites both without reconciling.

## Counter-arguments & Data Gaps

**Gartner is never read directly.** Every figure arrives via secondary coverage — refs [^14],
[^32], [^33], [^18]. None links to a Gartner publication, and Gartner research sits behind a
paywall, so none of these figures can be verified against source.

Forecast-of-forecasts is a weak evidentiary base for budget decisions. Ref [^14]'s own framing is
honest about this: the numbers are "actionable negotiation leverage rather than predictive
forecasting."

The talent-compression argument built on the 44% figure is an inference, not a finding. No source
here provides mid-market AI salary data, time-to-hire, or offer-decline rates — the things that
would actually show compression.
